Cash is reality: working capital, the cost of waiting, and where profit should go

Companies rarely fail because the P&L shows a loss. They fail because the cash runs out. How the cash cycle works, what it costs at today's rates, and how to decide between paying suppliers early, collecting faster, repaying debt or returning cash.

Key points

  • Profit is an opinion and cash is a fact. W.T. Grant, Carillion and Silicon Valley Bank all looked solid on paper shortly before they failed. What failed was cash.
  • Product and cash move on different clocks. In the example below, a toy company finances every order for 115 days, from paying its supplier to collecting from its customer.
  • At today's borrowing costs, every 10 days taken out of the cash cycle is worth real money. Early-payment discounts are usually worth taking as a buyer, and usually too expensive to offer as a seller.
  • Allocate cash in a fixed order: secure liquidity, repay expensive debt, invest where returns beat the cost of capital, then return the rest.

Most management reporting starts with the P&L. Most corporate failures start with the cash flow. A business can report rising profits, show a strong balance sheet and still be unable to pay its suppliers next month, because profit records value when it is earned while cash records it when it arrives. When the gap between the two grows faster than the funding behind it, the company runs out of time.

The details differ, but the lesson is the same. Grant's profits were tied up in customer credit and stock. Carillion's were supported by cash borrowed from its own suppliers. SVB's assets were sound in the long run but could not be turned into cash fast enough. In each case, the measure that mattered was not profit or net assets but the timing of cash.

Product and cash move on different clocks

To see where cash goes, follow a single order. Exhibit 1 tracks one order at Brightfield Toys, the fictional company I use for examples, from purchase order to collection. The top lane shows where the product is. The bottom lane shows when money leaves and enters the bank account.

Three points stand out. First, the supplier is paid on day 90, before the goods even reach our warehouse. Second, freight and duty are paid on arrival, two months before the toy is sold. Third, the customer has the product from day 145 but pays on day 205. For the 115 days between the first payment and the collection, Brightfield is financing the order itself.

The standard way to measure this is the cash conversion cycle: days of inventory plus days to collect, minus the days the business takes to pay its suppliers.

What the cash cycle costs at today's rates

Money has a price again. The ECB's deposit facility rate is 2.50%, and three-month Euribor was about 2.8% in November 2026. A mid-sized borrower paying a margin of around 2 points therefore funds working capital at roughly 4.8%.

For Brightfield, with €100m of revenue and €50m of product cost, the cycle ties up €24.0m in net working capital: €16.4m in receivables and €11.6m in inventory, less €4.1m owed to suppliers. At 4.8% that costs about €1.15m a year in interest. Measured against the full cost of capital, it is about €1.68m.

Collecting 10 days sooner is worth twice as much as the other two levers, because receivables carry the full selling price, margin included. That is why credit control deserves the same attention as cost control.

Receivables: allowances, credit losses and early-payment discounts

Receivables are where profit turns into cash, or fails to. Three things deserve finance's attention.

Customer deductions. Retailers often pay short, deducting promotional allowances, damaged goods or pricing disputes from the remittance. Each unresolved deduction is cash that has been earned in the P&L but not received. Agreeing allowances in writing, matching them to the trade terms and resolving disputes within weeks is one of the fastest ways to reduce days to collect.

Expected credit losses. Under IFRS 9, most companies use a simplified approach for trade receivables, often a provision matrix that applies a loss rate to each ageing bucket. It is also a useful management tool, because it puts a price on letting balances age.

The €200k of balances over 90 days overdue is 1% of the ledger but carries more than a third of the provision. Chasing those few accounts improves both cash and profit.

Offering early-payment discounts. A discount for early payment is a loan from the seller to the customer at an implied interest rate. The formula is: discount ÷ (100 − discount) × 365 ÷ (days paid early). On common terms the rates are high, as Exhibit 5 shows.

As a seller, offering 2/10 net 60 costs about 15% a year, three times Brightfield's borrowing cost. It is worth offering only if the business is short of cash and cannot borrow, or if the discount meaningfully cuts credit losses on a risky customer. In most cases, cheaper bank finance or receivables finance is the better answer.

Payables: take the discount, but do not squeeze suppliers

The same arithmetic works the other way. As a buyer, if a supplier offers 2/10 net 30, paying early earns the equivalent of about 37% a year. Any company that can borrow at 4.8% should take that discount every time. Only the cheapest terms, such as 0.5/10 net 60, are not worth taking at today's rates.

Extending payment terms is the other lever, and it is where Carillion's story is a warning. In the EU, the Late Payment Directive limits terms between businesses to 60 days unless both sides expressly agree longer terms that are not grossly unfair to the supplier. Supplier finance, also called reverse factoring, can help suppliers get paid early at the buyer's credit rating, but it is a form of financing. Since 2024, IAS 7 and IFRS 7 require companies to disclose these arrangements, precisely because they can hide how much a company really depends on them.

The cost of capital: what each source of cash really costs

To decide what to do with cash, finance needs a hurdle rate. The weighted average cost of capital (WACC) blends the cost of equity and the after-tax cost of debt in proportion to how the company is funded. The cost of equity is usually estimated with the capital asset pricing model (CAPM): the risk-free rate, plus beta multiplied by the equity risk premium.

Two conclusions follow. Debt is much cheaper than equity after tax, which is why companies use it. But every extra unit of debt raises the risk for both lenders and shareholders, so the cheapest mix is not "all debt". And any use of cash, from holding inventory to buying a competitor, should earn more than the WACC.

These are the same tools that the CMA programme's Part 2, Strategic Financial Management, covers under corporate finance: working capital management, the cost of capital, capital structure and the choice between financing options. Studying them for the exam reinforced something I had seen in practice: the right answer always depends on the cost of the next euro of funding, not on the average.

Where to start

  1. Draw the product and cash timelines for your top products, as in Exhibit 1, and agree the cash conversion cycle by business unit.
  2. Put a euro value on 10 days of each lever at your current borrowing cost, and set targets for each.
  3. Review every early-payment discount you offer and every one you are offered against your borrowing cost.
  4. Run a weekly cash forecast for the next 13 weeks, alongside the monthly P&L forecast.
  5. Agree the cash allocation order with the board before the cash arrives, not after.

Profit tells you whether the business model works. Cash tells you whether the business will still be there to prove it.
