Gross-to-net, explained for people outside finance

A plain-English guide to the gap between list price and the money a company actually keeps.

Commercial teams often talk about gross sales. Finance talks about net revenue. The space between the two is called gross-to-net, and in consumer goods it can be very large. Understanding it is one of the fastest ways for anyone in a commercial role to have more influence on profit.

What sits in the gap

Gross sales are the value of what you ship at list price. From that, you deduct allowances: the discounts, promotions, rebates and other terms agreed with customers. What is left is net revenue, the money the business actually earns from the sale.

Why one point matters

Allowances are usually measured as a percentage of gross sales. One point sounds small, but it comes straight off net revenue, and most of it falls through to profit because the product cost has not changed. That is why finance watches this line so closely. Why it is hard to manage

  • It builds up over time. Each deal looks reasonable alone, and the total drifts upward.
  • It varies by channel. A domestic sale and a direct import or FOB sale carry very different terms, so channel mix moves the average.
  • It can be invisible. Some terms sit off-invoice, so the price on the invoice does not show the full picture.

A worked example

The easiest way to understand gross-to-net is to follow one sale. The example below starts from 100 of gross sales at list price and walks down to net revenue. The figures are illustrative, but the size of the gap is typical of many consumer goods categories.

Now add product cost of 40. Gross profit is 38. If allowances rise by one more point, from 22 to 23, net revenue falls to 77 and gross profit to 37. That single point has removed about 2.6% of gross profit, without any change in volume, price or cost. This is why a small drift in terms deserves attention.

The same arithmetic works in your favour. Recovering one point of allowances, for example by ending a rebate that no longer buys anything, adds the same 2.6% to gross profit. In many businesses, that is easier to achieve than a matching increase in volume, which would need extra stock, extra logistics and extra selling effort to deliver the same profit.

That is also why commercial teams benefit from seeing gross profit, not only net revenue, when they plan a deal.

A glossary of the gap

Different companies use different names, but the main types of allowance are much the same everywhere.

TermWhat it isWhere it sits
Standard discountA fixed reduction from list price for a customer or channelOn invoice
Promotional discountA temporary price reduction to support a promotionOn or off invoice
Volume rebateA payment when the customer reaches agreed volumesOff invoice, often at year-end
Co-op or A&P supportFunding for the customer's marketing of your productsOff invoice
Listing feeA payment to have a new product stockedOff invoice
Early-payment discountA reduction for paying before the due dateOn payment
Returns and damagesAllowances for unsold or damaged goodsOff invoice, as credit notes

How to manage it

Gross-to-net is managed through a few simple disciplines rather than one big project.

  • Keep a terms register. Every agreed term, for every customer, in one place, with its value, conditions and end date.
  • Set approval limits. The larger or more unusual the term, the more senior the approval.
  • Accrue off-invoice terms monthly. Rebates paid at year-end belong to the months in which the sales happened, or the P&L will flatter the year until the last month.
  • Evaluate promotions afterwards. Compare the extra volume with the cost, and stop the promotions that do not pay back.
  • Review the rate by customer every quarter. Look for drift, and ask what each customer delivers for its terms.

How it is reported

In management reports, gross sales and allowances are often shown separately, because that is how the business is managed. In published accounts, most of these terms are deducted from revenue: under IFRS 15, payments to customers generally reduce revenue unless they buy a distinct service at a fair price. That is one reason commercial teams and finance sometimes describe the same customer very differently. I go into how to set prices and terms in the first place in setting prices.

Three questions worth asking

  1. What do we get in return for each allowance? A term linked to performance is easier to justify than one that has simply always been there.
  2. Is this rate above or below similar customers and markets?
  3. Has the change in our rate come from real terms, or only from a shift in mix?

When sales and finance read this line together, conversations about pricing improve quickly. It is one of the areas where a finance partner can add the most value to a commercial team.
