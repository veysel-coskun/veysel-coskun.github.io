Writing commentary leaders actually read

A monthly pack is only useful if the reader knows what to do next. Here is the structure I use.

I have written and reviewed a lot of monthly performance packs, for Country Heads, regional leaders, private equity investors and Boards. The numbers are rarely the problem. The usual problem is that a reader finishes the page without knowing what changed, why, or what is needed from them.

Start with the answer

The first line should say what happened and how much it matters: "Net revenue is $2M behind forecast, mainly on lower volume in two markets, and the full-year outlook is at risk." A leader who reads nothing else should still have the main point.

Explain drivers, and skip the arithmetic

"Gross margin fell 40 basis points" repeats the table. A driver tells the reader something new: mix moved toward lower-margin channels, or a promotion ran longer than planned. Size each driver, so the reader sees which one matters most.

Separate risks from opportunities

A forecast is a judgement. I list risks and opportunities against it, each with an amount and an owner. It shows the range of possible outcomes and opens a useful conversation about which ones the team can influence.

End with a decision or an action

Every pack should close with what happens next: a decision needed, an action with a named owner, or a clear statement that no action is required. Commentary with no next step is only a description.

Size the drivers, then assign them

A variance only becomes useful when each driver has a size and an owner. A small table like this one, at the top of the commentary, often does more than a page of text.

DriverImpact vs forecastOwnerNext step
Lower volume in two markets−$1.4MMarket leadsRecovery plan by next review
Price and mix+$0.3MCommercial financeNone: favourable and expected to hold
Extended retailer promotion−$0.6MKey account managerRecover $0.3M at year-end settlement
Currency−$0.3MTreasuryNone: outside the team's control
Total−$2.0M

Sizing drivers takes a consistent method. Volume is usually measured at last period's or the forecast's average price, price and mix at current volume, and allowances and currency separately, so that the steps never double count. Agree the method once, document it, and use it every month. Readers quickly learn to trust a bridge that is built the same way each time, and they stop questioning the arithmetic and start discussing the business.

Notice that the drivers add up exactly to the total. If they do not, the reader will spend the meeting on the difference rather than on the decisions.

Write for the reader in front of you

The same numbers need different commentary for different readers. A board member, a general manager and a country sales head all read the same pack with different questions in mind. Good commentary starts from those questions rather than from the order of the P&L.

ReaderWhat they want to knowWhat to lead with
Board or investorsIs the plan on track, and what are the big risks?Full-year outlook, cash and the two or three issues that could change it
General managerWhere do I need to intervene?Drivers by market and brand, with owners and decisions needed
Country headHow is my market doing against its targets and peers?Their own drivers, compared with similar markets
Sales directorWhich customers and promotions are working?Volume, price and allowances by customer, in commercial language

A useful test is to ask what the reader would do differently after reading each sentence. If the answer is nothing, the sentence can usually go. This keeps commentary short without losing anything that matters, and it trains the writer to think like the reader.

A one-page structure

1

Headline

What happened, how much it matters and whether the outlook has changed, in one or two sentences.

2

Drivers

The three or four drivers that explain the change, each sized and in order of importance.

3

Risks and opportunities

What could move the forecast from here, with amounts and owners.

4

Decisions and actions

What the reader needs to decide or do, by when, and who follows up.

Three rules for the writing

  • Use the reader's words. Say "discounts" if your audience thinks in discounts.
  • Keep the same order every month, so people know where to look.
  • Cut anything that would not change a decision.

Generative AI can now draft much of this from a variance table, and I think that is a good use of it. The structure still has to come from finance, because knowing what matters to this reader, this month, takes judgement about the business.
