The pricing waterfall: where margin quietly leaks

Why I build a pricing waterfall before any negotiation, and how to read one.

A list price is a starting point. By the time a sale reaches the bottom line, it has passed through a series of discounts, rebates, promotions and terms. A pricing waterfall lays those steps out in order, so you can see what you really keep. McKinsey made the idea famous with the pocket price waterfall, which shows how much revenue a company really keeps from each transaction.

How to build one

  1. Start from list price or gross sales for one customer, channel or SKU group.
  2. Deduct each on-invoice discount as its own step.
  3. Deduct off-invoice items, such as rebates, promotional funding and payment terms.
  4. Arrive at net price, then take off product cost and other direct costs to reach margin.

What to look for

A single waterfall is useful. Comparing them is better. Set two similar customers side by side, or the same customer across two years, and the questions appear quickly. Why does one customer get a rebate the other does not? Why has a promotional step grown while volume stayed flat?

The most valuable finding is usually a term that nobody can explain. It was agreed years ago, the reason has gone, and it continues. Those are the easiest conversations to reopen.

The comparison raises the right questions immediately. Why does Customer B receive a larger rebate? What does Customer A do with its larger promotional budget? Is B's longer payment term written into the contract, or simply tolerated? None of these questions is visible from the invoice price.

Where margin usually leaks

In my experience the leaks tend to come from the same few places, and most of them are not decided in a negotiation at all.

LeakHow it happensFix
Legacy termsA discount agreed years ago for a reason that no longer appliesReview every term at least once a year
Unmanaged payment daysCustomers pay later than agreed and nobody follows upTrack actual payment days against terms
Free freightSmall or urgent orders delivered at no chargeMinimum order sizes and a delivery charge below them
Promotion overrunsA promotion runs longer or deeper than plannedFixed dates and post-promotion reviews
DeductionsCustomers deduct disputed amounts from paymentsFast dispute resolution and clear written terms

Building a waterfall from the ledger is usually easier than people expect. Gross sales and on-invoice discounts are already on the invoice lines. Rebates and promotions sit in accruals or credit notes, payment terms can be priced from the receivables ledger, and freight from logistics invoices. The work is in allocating them to customers and products consistently.

Agree the allocation rules with the commercial team before showing results. A waterfall that sales does not recognise will be argued about rather than used.

Start with the largest customers and the largest products. A waterfall for the top twenty customers usually covers most of the revenue, and it is enough to show where the conversation should go next.

The pocket price band

Plot the pocket price of every customer for the same product, and the result is usually a wide band rather than a single price. McKinsey's work on pricing calls this the pocket price band. A wide band is not wrong in itself, because customers differ in size and cost to serve, but each position on it should have a reason. Customers at the bottom of the band with no clear justification are where pricing conversations should start.

Using it in a negotiation

Before a negotiation, I want the commercial team to know three things: the current net position of this customer, how it compares with similar customers, and what each possible concession would cost. With a margin scenario model behind the waterfall, the team can test an offer in the room and see the effect at once.

Make it routine

A waterfall built once for a big negotiation helps. One that is refreshed regularly, by SKU and channel, becomes part of pricing governance. It is also a natural place for AI to help, by flagging customers or SKUs that drift away from the normal pattern.
