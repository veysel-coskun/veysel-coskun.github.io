AI in finance needs controls too

The discipline that keeps a month-end close reliable applies directly to AI.

I have spent much of my career around SOX-compliant controls, under US GAAP and IFRS, across many entities and markets. When finance teams begin using AI, I find that mindset carries over well. You do not need to invent a new way of thinking. You need to ask the questions a good controller already asks.

Formal guidance now points the same way. In February 2026 COSO published Achieving Effective Internal Control Over Generative AI, which applies its Internal Control framework to generative AI. Here are five questions I would put to any AI use in a finance process. This is a working view from practice, not compliance advice, so involve your own compliance and audit teams early.

1. Source: where did this come from?

Every figure in an AI output should trace back to a known system or report. If the tool cannot show its source, treat the output as a draft idea and keep it out of the numbers.

2. Review: who checked it?

Treat AI output the way you treat the work of a new analyst: useful, fast and subject to review. Decide who reviews what, and at what level of detail, based on how much the output matters.

3. Access: what can it see?

Know which data goes into which tool, and whether that tool is approved for it. Unpublished results, pricing and customer terms deserve particular care. The easiest control is often to give the tool only what the task needs.

4. Change: what happens when it changes?

A prompt that drives a monthly process is part of that process. If someone changes it, or the underlying model is updated, the output can change. Keep prompts under version control and re-test after a change, as you would with a report or a system configuration.

5. Evidence: could you show an auditor?

If AI contributed to a reported figure or a management decision, you should be able to show what went in, what came out and who approved it. If that record would be hard to produce, the process is not ready yet.

Not every use needs the same control

Applying the strictest controls to every use of AI would stop adoption. A simple tiering, based on what the output is used for, keeps effort in proportion to risk.

How this fits the controls you already have

Most companies do not need a separate framework for AI. The existing ones stretch to cover it. An AI tool used in a financial process is, in control terms, another application: it needs the same access controls, the same change management and the same evidence of review as any spreadsheet or system that touches reported numbers. Treating it that way has a practical advantage. Auditors, process owners and IT teams already understand those controls, so adoption is faster and the conversation starts from familiar ground.

Who does what

Controls fail most often at the hand-offs between teams. Being explicit about roles avoids the gap where everyone assumes someone else checked.

RoleResponsibility
Process ownerDecides where AI is used in the process, and owns the result as with any other step
ReviewerChecks outputs at the level the tier requires, and signs off
IT and securityApproves tools, manages access, and confirms how data is handled
Compliance and legalAdvise on regulation, such as data protection and the EU AI Act, and on contracts
Internal auditTests that the controls work in practice, not only on paper

Red flags to watch for

  • Numbers in a pack that nobody can trace to a system.
  • A personal AI account used for company data, because the approved tool is slower.
  • A prompt that "everyone uses" but nobody owns.
  • Outputs that changed after a tool update, and nobody noticed until a reviewer asked.
  • Review that has quietly become a glance, because the tool is usually right.

The last one is the hardest. The better a tool performs, the more tempting it becomes to stop checking it. A good control environment makes review a habit, not a reaction to a mistake.

A 30-day start

  1. Week 1: list where AI is already being used in the finance team. The answer is usually "more than we thought".
  2. Week 2: place each use in a tier, and agree which tools are approved for which data.
  3. Week 3: write a one-page standard covering the five questions, and add AI uses to the existing control framework.
  4. Week 4: train the team, then spot-check a sample of medium and high-tier outputs.

None of this should slow a team down. Clear rules usually help people adopt new tools with more confidence, because everyone knows what is allowed and who is accountable. In my experience that is what a good control environment does for any change.
