Forecast accuracy is a finance job: insight, gatekeeping and better inputs

Why finance belongs between demand and supply planning, and how a toy company can plan an uncertain fourth quarter with sell-in, sell-through and sell-out data.

Key points

  • A forecast decides how much stock is bought, how much cash is tied up and how much margin is at risk. That makes forecast accuracy a finance outcome, not only a planning metric.
  • Finance adds most value in three roles: it provides insight, it gatekeeps both the demand and the supply forecast, and it creates inputs that no one else owns, such as price, promotions and trade terms.
  • In a seasonal business, one tolerance level for every month is a mistake. Accept wider error when little is at stake, and tighten tolerance and review cadence as the peak approaches.
  • Q4 data always arrives late. Finance can turn sell-in, sell-through and sell-out into early signals, and use scenarios to decide how much inventory to build before the holidays.

Most companies treat forecast accuracy as a supply chain metric. It sits in the planning team's dashboard, it is reviewed in the S&OP meeting, and finance takes the resulting number into its own outlook. I think that is the wrong way round. Every forecast is a financial commitment in disguise: it decides how much inventory is bought, how much working capital is tied up, and how much margin will later be spent on markdowns or lost to empty shelves.

The stakes are well documented. McKinsey reports that AI-driven forecasting in supply chains can reduce forecast errors by 20 to 50 percent, and cut lost sales and product unavailability by up to 65 percent. Those gains only reach the P&L if someone connects the forecast to money. That is finance's job.

Where finance sits: between demand and supply

A sound forecast process has two halves. Sales and marketing build the demand forecast: what customers and consumers will buy. Supply chain builds the supply forecast: what will be produced or purchased, when and where. Finance belongs in the middle, with a gate on each side.

Gate 1 asks whether demand is credible. Does the sell-in plan match what consumers are actually buying? Are the promotions in the forecast funded in the budget? Is a customer being asked to take more stock than it can sell, or more than its credit limit allows?

Gate 2 asks whether the supply plan is affordable. What does the inventory build cost in cash? How much stock will be left if demand comes in low, and who pays for it? Is the plan buying flexibility where uncertainty is highest?

Three roles for finance

1. Provide insight. Finance sees the whole picture: price, mix, trade spend, customer health and cash. Its job is to explain why a number is moving and how far to trust it. A forecast that is 5% up on last year means little until you know whether that comes from volume, price or a single large promotion.

2. Gatekeep. Gatekeeping is not about saying no. It means insisting that every forecast has an owner, a set of stated assumptions and a test against history. Finance is well placed to do this because it has no volume target to defend.

3. Create valuable inputs. Several of the most important forecast drivers live in finance or pass through it: list price changes, the promotional calendar and its funding, trade terms, customer credit limits, product cost and exchange rates. If these reach the planners late or inconsistently, accuracy suffers regardless of the model.

Sell-in, sell-through and sell-out

For any company that sells through retailers, three measures do most of the work. They answer different questions, and a forecast built on only one of them will mislead.

MeasureWhat it isWhat it tells finance
Sell-inOur shipments to retailers and distributorsOur revenue. It can run ahead of real demand when retailers build stock.
Sell-outRetailers' sales to consumers, usually from point-of-sale dataTrue demand. It is the best guide to what retailers will reorder.
Sell-throughSell-out as a share of the stock available in a period (opening stock plus receipts)Whether stock on the shelf is moving. Low sell-through today means lower orders tomorrow.

Two further measures follow from these. Retailer stock is opening stock plus sell-in minus sell-out. Weeks or months of cover divides that stock by expected sell-out, and shows whether a customer is over- or under-stocked for what is coming.

A worked example: Brightfield Toys

Brightfield Toys is a fictional toy company, and every figure below is illustrative. Its units are indexed so that full-year consumer sell-out equals 100. Like the real toy market, it is heavily seasonal: Circana's Juli Lennett notes that the second half of the year accounts for more than 60 percent of annual US toy sales. At Brightfield, 72% of sell-out falls in the second half and 53% in the fourth quarter alone.

The gap between the two series is the whole story. Brightfield ships most heavily in August to October, as retailers build stock for the holidays, while consumers buy most heavily in November and December. Revenue and true demand peak in different quarters. A forecast that only tracks sell-in will look excellent in the third quarter, right up to the point where retailers stop reordering.

Exhibit 3 is the chart finance should watch most closely through the autumn. Retailers hold 36 units at the end of October, a little over two months of forward cover going into November. If consumer demand arrives as planned, that stock clears and the season ends at the target of 10. If it does not, the excess stays on retailers' shelves, and Brightfield pays for it twice: once in markdown support and again in lower orders the following spring.

Seasonality should change the tolerance, not just the forecast

Many companies hold every month to the same accuracy target. In a seasonal business that wastes effort in quiet months and allows too much error when it matters. A better approach sets tolerance and review cadence by phase of the season, as in Exhibit 4.

  • Off-season (January to June): 28% of the year's sell-out. A ±20% tolerance is acceptable because the money at stake is small, and monthly reviews are enough. The main job is clearing last season's stock and reading early signals on new lines.
  • Build (July to October): also 28% of sell-out, but this is when most of the year's stock is committed. Tolerance tightens to ±12% with fortnightly reviews, focused on retailer orders against sell-through and weeks of cover.
  • Peak (November and December): 44% of sell-out. Tolerance is ±8%, and reviews are weekly, using point-of-sale data by key account.

Calendar effects belong in the forecast too. In 2024, Thanksgiving fell on 28 November, leaving five fewer days between Thanksgiving and Christmas than in 2023. A forecast that compares weeks rather than shopping days will misread that as a fall in demand.

Why Q4 is ambiguous, and how finance creates the data

The difficulty is timing. Most of the inventory decision has to be made in the third quarter, but most of the evidence arrives in November and December. Finance cannot wait for certainty, so it has to build leading indicators from the data it already has:

  • Early sell-through on new items. September and October sell-through on this year's launches, compared with launches in previous years, is one of the best early guides to which lines will run hot.
  • The order book against sell-out. If retailers are ordering faster than consumers are buying, cover is rising. That may be a deliberate build, or an early sign of overstock.
  • Weeks of cover by key account. Averages hide problems. One large customer with ten weeks of cover can absorb a whole quarter's upside elsewhere.
  • Promotion and calendar effects. Known events, such as an October sales event or a late Thanksgiving, explain shifts that would otherwise look like changes in demand.

Then finance turns the uncertainty into scenarios. Starting from the end of September, Brightfield's plan has retailers holding 30 units, receiving 33 more in the fourth quarter and selling 53 to consumers.

The two risks are not symmetrical. The low case costs margin over several quarters, while the high case costs sales that cannot be recovered. That is why the right answer is rarely a single number. It is usually a committed core quantity, plus an option on the items with the most uncertainty.

Where AI and Power BI fit

None of this needs an expensive platform to start. A well-built Power BI report can hold sell-in, sell-out, retailer stock, sell-through and cover by customer and item in one place, with the forecast and its accuracy alongside. The Analytics pane can add a seasonal forecast line and anomaly detection to a time series, which makes a useful baseline to challenge.

AI tools extend this in three practical ways. They produce a statistical baseline quickly and at item level. They flag anomalies in point-of-sale data, such as a sudden spike at one retailer, before anyone reviews the numbers by hand. And they draft the first version of the variance commentary, so the team spends its time on judgement rather than on writing. People still own the assumptions, the overrides and the final number.

The way to keep everyone honest is to measure Forecast Value Added (FVA), an approach set out by Michael Gilliland. It compares each step of the process with a naive forecast, and asks whether that step actually improved accuracy. An illustrative result for Brightfield shows why the finance gate matters:

Forecast stepError (weighted MAPE)BiasValue added
Naive (same season last year)34%−2%Baseline
Statistical baseline (AI or Power BI)26%+1%+8 points against naive
Sales overrides29%+9%−3 points: the overrides made it worse
Consensus after the finance gates22%+3%+4 points against the statistical baseline

In this example, the sales overrides added optimism rather than accuracy. That is very common, and it is exactly what a gatekeeper should catch. Bias matters as much as error: a forecast that is consistently 9% high is more expensive than one that is randomly 9% out in both directions, because it keeps adding inventory.

Where to start

  1. Put sell-in, sell-out, retailer stock and sell-through in one report, by key account.
  2. Set tolerance and review cadence by phase of the season, not one target for the year.
  3. Formalise the two gates: demand credibility and supply affordability, each with a named owner.
  4. Build Q4 scenarios by the end of the third quarter, with a committed core and a flexible upside.
  5. Measure Forecast Value Added and bias for every step, and stop the steps that do not add value.

Forecast accuracy improves fastest when finance stops treating the forecast as an input to its own numbers and starts treating it as a decision it shares responsibility for.
