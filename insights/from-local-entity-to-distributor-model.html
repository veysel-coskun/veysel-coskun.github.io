From local entity to distributor: a finance checklist

What finance needs to get right when a market moves to a distributor operating model.

Moving a loss-making local entity to a distributor model is one of the more demanding changes a finance leader can support. I have worked through this in several markets. The spreadsheet is the easy part. Most of the value, and the risk, sits in how the transition is run.

Build the case on the full picture

Compare the two models on the same basis. A distributor model usually means lower net revenue per unit, because the distributor needs a margin, but it can remove a large fixed cost base. The right comparison is profit and cash after all costs, including one-off exit costs, over several years.

Decide what you keep

Define the retained roles early. Someone must still own the brand, the pricing principles, the commercial plan and the performance of the partner. Unclear accountability is one of the most common reasons a transition disappoints.

Reset pricing and trade terms

A move from domestic sales to a distributor changes the gross-to-net structure. Set clear pricing principles and allowance rules from the start, and check how the new prices relate to neighbouring markets.

Plan the exit carefully

The checklist covers people, contracts, inventory, receivables, tax and legal entity matters. Bring HR, legal and tax in at the start. Inventory and receivables deserve special attention, because they turn into cash or write-offs during the handover.

Keep visibility after go-live

Once you stop selling directly, you can lose sight of the market. Agree reporting with the partner on sell-out, inventory and activity, and review it regularly. Good governance of a distributor looks a lot like good governance of a shared service: clear service levels, regular reviews and a named owner.

How the P&L changes

The shape of the market P&L changes completely when a distributor takes over. Revenue falls, because the distributor needs a margin, but most of the local cost base disappears. The illustrative example below compares the two models for the same consumer demand.

The example also shows why the decision needs a multi-year view. One-off exit costs come first, and the benefit builds over time. There is a cash effect too: inventory and customer receivables move to the distributor, which releases working capital, while the company takes on credit risk on a single customer, the distributor itself.

Choosing the right partner

The distributor becomes the company's face in the market, so the selection deserves the same care as an acquisition. Price and margin matter, but they are rarely what makes a partnership fail. These are the criteria I would weigh:

CriterionWhat to look for
Customer accessStrong relationships with the retailers that matter in the category
Financial strengthEnough capital to hold seasonal stock and carry customer credit
Brand fitA portfolio that complements rather than competes with your brands
CapabilitySales, logistics and marketing skills, and the systems to report sell-out
Reputation and complianceClean history on ethics, tax and trade compliance

Due diligence on a distributor should include its financial statements, references from retailers and the quality of its reporting. A partner that cannot tell you what it sold last month will not be able to help you plan next year.

A transition in six steps

1

Decide and build the case

Compare both models over several years, on profit and cash, including exit costs and risks.

2

Select and contract the partner

Assess capability, finances and reputation. Agree pricing, terms, service levels, reporting and exit clauses.

3

Prepare the exit

People, contracts, leases, tax and the legal entity, with HR, legal and tax involved from the start.

4

Transfer stock and customers

Agree the value of inventory taken over, collect or transfer receivables, and introduce the partner to key customers.

5

Go live and stabilise

Watch service levels, pricing and stock closely for the first months, and fix issues fast.

6

Govern with a scorecard

Review the partner monthly on a small set of agreed measures.

Communication runs through every step. Employees, customers, suppliers and the distributor's own team all need to hear the same message at the right time. Finance has a particular role here, because customers will ask practical questions about invoices, credit notes, open orders and payment details. A short, agreed customer letter and a single contact point for the first weeks avoid most of the confusion.

Plan for the first year-end as well. Rebates, returns and credit notes that relate to sales made before the transition often arrive months later, and someone needs to own them after the local team has gone.

A distributor scorecard

MeasureWhy it matters
Sell-out growthShows whether consumers are buying, not just whether the distributor is ordering

Payment days and credit useThe distributor is now your largest credit exposure in the market

Done well, this kind of change can turn a loss-making position into a profitable one quite quickly. It rests on clear numbers, clear roles and steady communication with everyone affected.
