Setting prices: markup, market, competitive and target pricing, and what they do to the P&L

Where to start with pricing, what belongs in a price, and why one list price with pay-for-performance terms usually beats a separate net price for every customer.

Key points

  • Price is the most powerful profit lever most companies have, and the least managed. Start from what the market will pay, not from what the product costs.
  • Use markup as a floor and a sense check, market and competitive pricing to set the level, and target pricing to decide what the product is allowed to cost.
  • A price is a bundle. Incoterms, risk, payment terms and trends all change what a price really includes, so two equal-looking prices can be worth very different amounts.
  • Prefer one list price per market inside a corridor, with discounts paid only for something the customer does for you. In the example below, the same net price earns €71k more a year when the terms buy real services.

Pricing gets less attention than cost in most finance teams, even though it usually matters more. McKinsey's classic analysis, The power of pricing, found that for an average S&P 1500 company a 1% price increase, with volume unchanged, would raise operating profit by about 8%. The same arithmetic works against you: the authors calculated that volume would have to rise by 18.7% just to offset a 5% price cut.

So the question is not only "what should the price be?" It is also "how do we decide, what does the price include, and how do we keep it from leaking away?" I have written before about where margin leaks between list price and pocket price and about gross-to-net. This article covers the step before both: setting the price in the first place.

Four ways to set the level

MethodHow it worksBest used whenWatch out for
Markup (cost-plus)Cost plus a fixed percentageCustom or contract work, commodities, or when there is no market referenceIt ignores what customers will pay, and passes your inefficiency on to them
Market (consumer) pricingStart from the price the end customer will pay, then work backBranded consumer products with clear price pointsIt needs good consumer and channel data
Competitive pricingSet a price index against named competitorsMature categories where shoppers compare directlyFollowing a rival's mistakes, and price wars
Target pricingFix the price and margin first, and design the product to the cost that is leftNew products, before cost is locked inIt needs engineering, sourcing and finance to work together early

Markup is a floor, not a strategy. It is simple and easy to defend, and it guarantees that every sale covers cost. But it says nothing about value. Watch the language too: markup is calculated on cost, while margin is calculated on price. A 50% markup is only a 33% margin, and a 100% markup is a 50% margin. Many pricing arguments are really two people using different bases.

Market pricing starts with the shopper. For a consumer product, the shelf price is the anchor. Toys, for example, cluster around familiar price points such as €19.99 or €29.99, and a product priced just above a threshold can sell far less than one just below it. Finance's job is to work backwards from that price point through VAT and the retailer's margin to the price we can actually invoice.

Competitive pricing keeps you honest. A price index against two or three named competitors shows whether you are premium, at par or discounted, and whether that position is deliberate. It is a check on the market price rather than a method in its own right.

Target pricing turns the logic around. Instead of asking "what does it cost, so what should we charge?", it asks "what will the market pay, so what are we allowed to spend?" The difference is that cost becomes a design target. That conversation is far more productive before a product is tooled than after.

Where to start: outside in, then inside out

My recommended order is: set the level from the market, check it against competitors, work back to the cost the product is allowed to have, and use markup only to confirm that every channel still clears a minimum margin. Exhibit 2 shows the walk-back for a single toy from Brightfield Toys, the fictional company I use for examples.

Of a €29.99 shelf price, Brightfield invoices €14.86 after all trade terms, and needs a landed cost of €7.30 to earn its target gross margin of about 50%. If the factory quote comes in at €8.50, the answer is not to raise the price to €34.99. It is to redesign the product or the packaging, or accept a lower margin knowingly.

What goes into the price

Two prices with the same number on the invoice can be worth very different amounts. Before comparing prices across customers or markets, finance needs to know what each one includes.

Incoterms decide who pays for delivery and who carries the risk. The International Chamber of Commerce's Incoterms rules set out where costs and risk pass from seller to buyer. The further along the chain the seller delivers, the more cost the price has to cover.

IncotermSeller delivers toRisk passesSeller's cost, € per unit
EXW Ex WorksIts own premisesWhen goods are made available6.10
FCA Free CarrierThe buyer's carrier, cleared for exportOn handover to the carrier6.20
FOB Free On BoardOn board the ship at the port of loadingWhen the goods are on board6.25
CIF Cost, Insurance and FreightThe destination port, with insuranceOn board at the port of loading6.73
DAP Delivered At PlaceThe named destination, not cleared for importAt the destination6.98
DDP Delivered Duty PaidThe named destination, with import duty paidAt the destination7.53

The same product needs a price about 23% higher under DDP than under Ex Works just to earn the same margin. Note the CIF trap as well: the seller pays for freight and insurance to the destination, but the risk passes to the buyer when the goods are loaded at origin.

Risk belongs in the price. Currency exposure, customer credit risk, payment terms, return rights and volume commitments all have a cost. A customer that pays in 90 days, returns unsold stock and buys in a weak currency should not get the same price as one that pays in 30 days and takes the stock risk itself.

Trends move the floor. Raw material costs, freight rates, tariffs, exchange rates and wage inflation change what the product costs to deliver. Consumer trends move the ceiling: what is in demand, and which price points shoppers accept. A good pricing process reviews both at least once a year, and has an agreed trigger for an earlier review.

One price for all, or different prices?

There are two broad models. In the first, each customer gets its own net price, often negotiated over years. In the second, there is one list price per market, and customers earn discounts by doing things that create value for the supplier. I strongly prefer the second, for three reasons. It is transparent, so price differences can be explained. It is conditional, so a discount can be withdrawn if the service stops. And it makes the trade-off visible: you can see what each discount buys.

Different prices across regions are often justified by different costs, duties, currencies and channel structures. The risk is arbitrage. If the gap between neighbouring markets is wider than the cost of moving the goods, stock will flow from the cheap market to the expensive one, and the higher price will not hold. The usual answer is a price corridor: a band around a reference price within which each country's list price must sit.

Competition law sets limits here, and pricing teams should take legal advice before designing their structure. Under the EU's Vertical Block Exemption Regulation, for example, a supplier may recommend a resale price but may not fix the price its retailers charge, and restrictions on retailers serving customers in other markets are treated very strictly. A corridor manages the supplier's own prices. It does not control what retailers do with theirs.

Pay for performance: what a discount should buy

Every trade term should answer one question: what does the customer do for us in return, and is it worth more than it costs? Typical examples are:

TermExample sizeWhat the customer does in return
On-invoice standard discount5%None: simply lowers the price
A&P support (co-op marketing)4%Promotional space, catalogue and online features
Central delivery allowance3%Delivery to one distribution centre instead of stores
Data-sharing allowance1%Weekly sell-out and stock data by store
Early-payment discount (2% at 10 days)2%Payment in 10 days instead of 60
Volume growth rebate2%Growth above last year, paid only if achieved

Two of these deserve a closer look. Data sharing is worth more than most companies assume, because store-level sell-out data is what makes an accurate forecast possible, as I described in the finance role in forecast accuracy. Early-payment discounts are often worth less. Two percent for paying 50 days earlier is an annual rate of about 15%. If your cost of capital is 8%, that discount costs far more than it saves.

What it does to the P&L

Exhibit 4 compares two ways of selling the same product to the same retailer at the same net price of €14.86 a unit. On the left, Brightfield charges a €17.90 list price and gives 17% of terms, each tied to a service. On the right, it simply agrees a net price of €14.86 with no conditions, as many companies do after years of negotiation.

Net revenue and gross profit are identical. The difference appears below gross profit. Under the pay-for-performance model, the retailer takes delivery at one distribution centre, pays in 10 days and shares its data. Under the net-price model, Brightfield delivers to every store, waits 60 days for payment and gets no data. Customer contribution is €71k a year higher with the terms, about 4.8 points of net revenue, and that is before any value is put on the data or the promotional support.

Two accounting points matter when you present this. First, "gross sales" is a management measure. Under IFRS 15, consideration payable to a customer reduces revenue unless it pays for a distinct service at no more than its fair value, so most of these terms sit between gross sales and reported revenue. Second, because the terms are visible, they can be measured. A term that does not buy its service is a price cut, and should be negotiated as one.

Where AI and analytics help

Pricing is a good area for analytics, because the data is already in the ledger. A Power BI price waterfall by customer shows who receives which terms and what they deliver in return. AI tools can estimate price elasticity from promotional history, monitor competitors' online prices, flag deals that fall outside the corridor, and draft the commercial case for a price increase. The decision on the price itself, and the conversation with the customer, stay with people.

Where to start

  1. Build a price waterfall for your top customers, from list price to contribution, including logistics and payment terms.
  2. For each product, write down the consumer price point and work back to the allowable cost.
  3. Agree one list price per market, inside a corridor, and document the reasons for every gap.
  4. Link every trade term to a service, a measure and an owner. Stop paying for services that are not delivered.
  5. Price risk explicitly: Incoterms, payment terms, currency and returns.

The goal is not the highest possible price. It is a price that reflects the value the customer receives, covers the cost and risk of serving them, and holds up when someone in another market or another customer asks why it is different.
