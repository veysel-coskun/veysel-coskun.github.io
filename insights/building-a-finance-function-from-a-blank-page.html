Building a finance function from a blank page

What I would do first, and in what order, when there is no finance team, process or system yet.

I joined a new emerging markets business unit at its start, with no finance infrastructure, processes or team in place. Few finance leaders get to start from nothing, and it teaches you what really matters. If I did it again, this is the order I would follow.

1. Agree what the business needs to know

Before any system or template, ask the general manager which decisions finance must support. In a distributor portfolio that meant profitability by market, customer and brand, and clear visibility of trade spend and inventory. The answers drive everything that follows.

2. Design the structure

Next come the foundations: the chart of accounts, cost centres and reporting hierarchies. These feel technical, but they decide what you can report for years. Getting them right before system configuration is much cheaper than fixing them later.

3. Set up controls early

Bring in compliance from the start. An approvals framework and clear close routines are easy to build into a new process, and painful to add once people have developed their own habits.

4. Create the rhythm

A business runs on its calendar. Set the month-end cadence, the performance review, and the budget and forecast cycles, and keep to them. Consistency builds trust faster than any single report.

5. Automate as you go

Begin with simple templates and move to dashboards once the data flows are stable. Standardising first means you automate a good process, not a messy one.

6. Build the team around partnering

Hire and coach people who enjoy the business as much as the numbers. A new function has a rare chance to make business partnering the norm from the first day.

The first 100 days

The six steps above happen in a rough order. In practice, a new finance function can usually move from nothing to a reliable monthly rhythm in about three months, if the work is phased like this:

The core documents

A handful of documents carry most of the agreements a finance function depends on. Writing them early is far easier than reconstructing them later from habits.

DocumentWhy it matters
Chart of accounts and hierarchiesDecides what can be reported, by whom and at what level, for years
Delegation of authoritySays who can approve what, and is the backbone of internal control
Close calendar and checklistTurns the month-end into a repeatable process
Accounting policies and key judgementsKeeps treatment consistent as people change

Shaping the team

A new finance function is usually small, so every role has to cover more than one area. A structure that works for a growing business unit looks something like this, with people added as the business grows.

RoleFocus
Head of financePartner to the general manager, owner of the numbers, the controls and the plan
ControllerClose, balance sheet, policies, audits and compliance
FP&A and commercial financeBudget, forecast, performance reviews, pricing and trade terms
Reporting and systemsData flows, dashboards, master data and automation

Shared services or group teams can handle transaction processing, so the local team can focus on control and partnering. The important thing is that each area has a named owner from the start, even when one person owns several.

Hiring order matters as much as the structure. In a new unit I would bring in controllership capacity first, because the close and the controls have to work before anything else can be trusted. Commercial finance follows closely, because that is where the business feels the value of finance soonest. Reporting and systems skills can often be borrowed from a group team at the start and built locally later, once the data flows are stable.

Whatever the order, write a short role description for every position before hiring, so the gaps and overlaps are visible early.

What good looks like after a year

  • The close is on the same day every month, and the numbers rarely change afterwards.
  • Leaders use the management pack in their reviews, rather than their own spreadsheets.
  • The budget and forecast follow a calendar everyone knows.
  • Controls are in place and tested, and the first audit raises no major points.
  • The finance team is invited to commercial decisions, not just informed of them.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Buying the system before designing the structure. The system will then decide your structure for you.
  • Copying another unit's set-up without asking why. A distributor business and a direct business need different views.
  • Building reports nobody asked for. Start from the decisions, not from the data that happens to exist.
  • Hiring only for technical skill. A new function needs people who can partner with the business from day one.
  • Leaving controls until later. Later is always harder, because habits have formed.

The biggest lesson is that a finance function is mostly a set of agreements: on definitions, ownership, timing and standards. Systems record those agreements, so it pays to make them carefully.
