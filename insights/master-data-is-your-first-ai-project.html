Master data is your first AI project

Before asking AI for insight, make sure your customers, SKUs and prices mean the same thing everywhere.

When I helped build a finance function from a blank page across more than 20 markets, some of the most important decisions were also the least glamorous: the chart of accounts, the cost centre structure, the reporting hierarchies, and who owned customer, vendor, SKU, pricing and costing data.

None of that looks like an AI project, yet it decides whether one will work.

Why AI raises the stakes

A person who sees two versions of a customer name will usually notice. A model may simply treat them as two customers, then give you a confident answer about both. Weak master data was always an inconvenience. With AI it can produce a clear, well-written and wrong conclusion, and that is harder to spot.

Four questions to ask first

  1. Who owns each data set? Customer, vendor, SKU, pricing and costing each need a named owner and a clear process for changes.
  2. Is there one hierarchy? Market, channel, brand and product groups should roll up the same way in every report. If each region uses its own version, cross-market analysis is unreliable.
  3. Do definitions match? Gross sales, allowances and variable margin must mean the same thing in every market. Otherwise a comparison mixes a real change with a change of method.
  4. Can you tie back? Every dashboard total should reconcile to the ledger. If it does not, fix that before adding any layer on top.

Who owns what

Ownership is the control that makes all the others work. A practical split looks like this, though the names of the teams will vary.

Measure data quality like any other KPI

  • Completeness: the share of records with every mandatory field filled.
  • Duplicates: the number of suspected duplicate customers, vendors and SKUs.
  • Timeliness: how long a change request takes from request to approval.
  • Reconciliation: whether dashboard totals tie to the ledger every month.

Report these monthly to the same people who receive the financial results. Data quality improves fastest when its owners can see it.

Making the business case

Master data work is hard to fund because its benefit is indirect. The way to make the case is to show its cost in terms leaders already care about. How many hours does the team spend each month reconciling reports that should agree? How often has a customer's profitability been wrong because its sales were split across records? How many invoices were issued at an expired price? Each of these has a number, and together they usually justify the work many times over. Add the AI argument on top: the better the data, the more of the analysis can be trusted to automated tools, and the faster the team can answer new questions.

Where AI can help with the data itself

There is a useful twist here. The same tools that need clean data are good at helping to clean it. Language models are well suited to suggesting which customer records are probably duplicates, proposing a brand or category for a new SKU from its description, and flagging prices whose validity dates overlap. The rule is the same as anywhere else in finance: AI suggests, the data owner approves, and every change is logged.

A four-week plan for one question

1

Week 1: choose the question

Pick one valuable, recurring question, such as margin by brand and market, and list every data set the answer depends on.

2

Week 2: find the gaps

Check duplicates, missing fields and mismatched hierarchies in those data sets only. Ignore everything else for now.

3

Week 3: fix and assign owners

Clean the records, agree the definitions, and give each data set a named owner and a change process.

4

Week 4: answer and reconcile

Produce the answer, reconcile it to the ledger, and show the business what changed because the data was fixed.

Start small

A company does not need perfect data everywhere before it starts. Pick one valuable question, such as margin by brand and market, and clean the data behind that answer. You get a useful result quickly, and you show the business why the groundwork matters.

This is the less exciting side of AI in finance, but it is often what separates a good demonstration from a tool people trust every month. Teams that invest in structure now will get far more from AI later.
